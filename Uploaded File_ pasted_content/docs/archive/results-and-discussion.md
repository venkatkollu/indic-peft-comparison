Results

Data-efficiency curves. Figure 1 shows macro F1 as a function of training budget for each PEFT method, plotted separately for Hindi and Telugu. All three methods follow the expected shape — near-chance performance at the smallest budgets (F1 ≈ 0.17–0.22 at 50–100 samples, close to the 0.167 random-chance floor for 3-way classification), rising to substantially above-chance performance at the largest budget tested (F1 = 0.59–0.68 at 20,000 samples).

IA³ consistently outperforms LoRA and DoRA from budget = 500 onward, in both languages. At budget = 500, IA³ reaches F1 = 0.401 (Hindi) and 0.382 (Telugu), compared to F1 ≈ 0.24 for both LoRA and DoRA in both languages — roughly a 0.14–0.16 absolute F1 advantage. This gap persists at every subsequent budget: at 2,000 samples, IA³ leads by 0.22 F1 in Hindi (0.548 vs. 0.321–0.323) and by 0.11–0.14 F1 in Telugu (0.399 vs. 0.284–0.286). At the largest budget (20,000), the gap narrows but IA³ retains a lead in Hindi (0.676 vs. 0.630–0.633) and in Telugu (0.632 vs. 0.587–0.591). Because this ordering holds independently in both Hindi (Indo-Aryan, Devanagari script) and Telugu (Dravidian, Telugu script), the IA³ advantage does not appear to be script-specific — it replicates across two typologically and orthographically distinct languages.

LoRA and DoRA are statistically indistinguishable at every budget tested. Across all twelve (language × budget) cells excluding the two smallest, LoRA and DoRA differ by at most 0.02 F1, and their 95% confidence intervals overlap substantially in every case (e.g., at budget = 20,000, Hindi: LoRA 0.633 ± 0.004 vs. DoRA 0.630 ± 0.002). DoRA's added magnitude-direction decomposition produces no measurable accuracy benefit over plain LoRA in this setting, despite incurring higher trainable-parameter count (906K vs. 888K) and the longest total training time of the three methods (Table 2).

Ranking agreement across languages. Using the best-performing method at each budget as the unit of comparison, IA³ is the top performer at 4 of 6 budgets in both Hindi and Telugu (500, 1000, 2000, and 20000), giving 100% ranking agreement at the budgets where a clear winner emerges. At the two smallest budgets (50, 100), all three methods are statistically indistinguishable from chance and from each other, so no meaningful ranking exists to compare.

Stability at small budgets. IA³'s advantage comes with a caveat: it shows a markedly wider confidence interval at small-to-moderate budgets than LoRA or DoRA — e.g., at Telugu budget = 1000, IA³'s 95% CI is ±0.123 (mean F1 = 0.290) versus ±0.087 for DoRA and ±0.059 for LoRA at the same budget. Of 108 total training runs, 13 (12%) failed to learn entirely (accuracy at exact chance level, 0.333); 9 of these 13 failures occurred in IA³ specifically, concentrated at the 50- and 100-sample budgets. We attribute this to IA³'s selected learning rate (5×10⁻³, chosen by a pooled hyperparameter search) being fifty times larger than LoRA/DoRA's (1×10⁻⁴) — a rate that supports faster adaptation at moderate-to-large budgets but appears to occasionally destabilize training when only 50–100 gradient-informative examples are available.

Compute efficiency. IA³ uses the fewest trainable parameters of the three methods (657K vs. 888K for LoRA and 906K for DoRA) but does not translate this into the fastest wall-clock training time in our implementation, likely reflecting the computational structure of its rescaling operations rather than a fundamental efficiency disadvantage (Table 2, compute_efficiency.csv).

Discussion

The central finding — that IA³ outperforms both LoRA and DoRA once training data exceeds roughly 500 examples, and that this ordering is stable across two languages from different script families — runs counter to the common default of treating LoRA as the safe, general-purpose choice for PEFT. In our setting, IA³'s much smaller set of trainable parameters (rescaling vectors rather than low-rank matrices) appears sufficient, and possibly even advantageous, for adapting a multilingual encoder to a classification task once a moderate amount of task-specific signal is available. This is consistent with the original IA³ paper's motivation — that multiplicative rescaling can be a more parameter-efficient inductive bias than additive low-rank updates for certain adaptation regimes — but to our knowledge this is the first time the comparison has been extended to a non-Latin-script, low-resource Indic setting with a systematic budget sweep.

The absence of any measurable difference between LoRA and DoRA is a notable negative result. DoRA's magnitude-direction decomposition was proposed to improve on LoRA's learning capacity, and prior work reports DoRA advantages on English-centric benchmarks; we do not observe that advantage here. One plausible explanation is that the classification head — not the encoder adapters — is the primary bottleneck at small-to-moderate budgets in our setup (both methods must simultaneously learn a randomly initialized head, discussed further in docs/methodology-notes.md), which may compress any encoder-level differences between LoRA and DoRA that would otherwise be visible.

The instability observed in IA³ at the smallest budgets is worth treating as a substantive finding rather than an artifact to be engineered away. A learning rate tuned for good performance at moderate-to-large budgets (our pooled search selected 5×10⁻³ using budget = 2000 as the representative size) is not guaranteed to be stable at 50–100 samples, where a small number of noisy or unrepresentative gradient steps can dominate training. This suggests that any practical guidance drawn from this study — "IA³ is the strongest PEFT method for Hindi/Telugu NLI" — should be qualified by budget: IA³ appears to be the preferred choice once a few hundred labeled examples are available, but LoRA's comparatively lower variance at 50–100 samples may make it the safer choice in extremely low-resource annotation settings.

Two limitations bound how far these conclusions generalize. First, "high-resource" in this study means 20,000 examples, not the full ~392,702-example training set, for compute reasons documented in docs/methodology-notes.md; whether the IA³ advantage persists, narrows, or reverses at the true full-data scale is untested. Second, learning rates were searched once per method, pooled across both languages, to keep the cross-language comparison uncontaminated by per-language LR tuning — this is methodologically appropriate for our research question but means the reported numbers are not necessarily each method's absolute best-achievable performance in either language individually.
